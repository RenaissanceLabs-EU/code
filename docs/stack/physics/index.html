Transistors: switches made of matter

How can a piece of sand hold a yes or a no?

At the bottom of the stack there is no code. There is a transistor: a switch with no moving parts, in which a voltage on one terminal decides whether current may flow between the other two. On and off, 1 and 0. A current processor holds tens of billions of them, and the largest chips hundreds of billions on a piece of silicon the size of a fingernail.

Everything above this level is an arrangement of these switches and a pattern of charge held in them. When a program runs, charge moves. That is the whole physical story of software.

The important idea at this level is the digital abstraction. Real voltages are never exactly high or exactly low, but each circuit pushes whatever it receives firmly to one side or the other before passing it on. Small errors are erased at every step instead of piling up. This is why a bit can be copied a trillion times and still be exactly the same bit, why software can be duplicated without loss, and why a computation can run for billions of steps and stay exact. Analog machines cannot do this, and it is the property the rest of the stack depends on.

The costs are physical too. Every switch uses energy and gives off heat, and transistors are now so small that the atomic structure of the material sets limits.

Four transistors make a decision

The most common kind of chip pairs two types of transistor. One type conducts when its control input is 1, and the other conducts when its input is 0. Connect two of the first kind in a chain to ground, and two of the second kind side by side to the supply, and the output is 0 only when both inputs are 1.

That circuit is a NAND gate: "not both". It takes four transistors, and the next level up shows that everything else can be built from it.

What to notice
  • For every combination of inputs, the output is connected to the supply or to ground, never both and never neither. That is what keeps the signal clean.
  • Only with A and B both at 1 is there a path to ground.
  • Almost no current flows from supply to ground once the output has settled, only a small leak. That is why this design, called CMOS, can pack billions of switches together.

Central ideas

Transistor
A switch controlled by a voltage, with no moving parts.
Bit
One binary choice, 0 or 1. In a chip it is a voltage or a stored charge.
CMOS
The standard chip technology, which pairs two complementary kinds of transistor so that one is off whenever the other is on.
Digital abstraction
Treating a range of voltages as simply 0 or 1, and restoring the signal at every stage so errors do not build up.
Clock
A signal that ticks at a steady rate and keeps all parts of a chip in step.
Moore's law
Gordon Moore's 1965 observation that the number of components on a chip was doubling at a regular rate.
Lithography
The process of printing circuit patterns onto silicon with light.

Landmarks

  1. 1947
    Point-contact transistor John Bardeen and Walter Brattain, Bell Labs

    On December 16, 1947, Bardeen and Brattain got a germanium point-contact device to amplify a signal (demonstrated to lab officials on December 23), giving computing a solid-state amplifier; the year refers to these December 1947 events, and the junction transistor was conceived separately in 1948. source

  2. 1958
    All-semiconductor integrated circuit Jack Kilby (Texas Instruments, 1958) and Robert Noyce (Fairchild, 1959)

    Kilby built a working oscillator with all components formed in semiconductor material on September 12, 1958, still wired with fine gold flying wires, and Noyce conceived the monolithic version in 1959 (patent filed July 1959, issued 1961) with metal lines over the protective oxide, which made putting a whole circuit on one chip manufacturable. source

  3. 1959
    Planar process Jean Hoerni, Fairchild Semiconductor

    Hoerni kept the oxide layer on the silicon wafer to protect the p-n junctions (idea recorded December 1957, patent disclosure January 1959, working planar transistor March 1959, the year used here), which cut leakage and let every part of a chip be built from one side of the wafer. source

  4. 1960
    MOS field-effect transistor Mohamed Atalla and Dawon Kahng, Bell Labs

    Atalla and Kahng built the first successful insulated-gate field-effect transistor in 1959 (the Computer History Museum milestone is dated 1960) by using thermally grown silicon dioxide to reduce surface states, the device structure that later chips are built from at enormous scale. source

  5. 1965
    Moore's 1965 projection Gordon Moore

    In the April 19, 1965 issue of Electronics, Moore projected that components per integrated circuit would double about every year, reaching roughly 65,000 by 1975, and at the 1975 IEEE International Electron Devices Meeting he revised the pace to a doubling every two years. source

  6. 1974
    Dennard scaling rules Robert Dennard and colleagues, IBM

    The 1974 IBM paper showed that shrinking a MOS transistor's horizontal dimensions by a factor also raises its speed by that factor and linked the shrink to lower power(similar scaling principles appeared in 1972 papers by Hoeneisen and Mead and by Dennard's team). source

  7. 2019
    EUV lithography in volume production TSMC (N7+ process), using ASML EUV tools

    TSMC announced on October 7, 2019 that its N7+ process, in volume production since the second quarter of 2019, was delivering customer products using extreme ultraviolet lithography, which it called the industry's first commercially available EUV technology (TSMC's own claim). source

Open problems and frontiers

  • Quantum error correction at scale

    Google Quantum AI reported in 2024 a distance-7 surface code (101 qubits) whose logical error rate falls by a factor of 2.14 each time the code distance grows by two, with logical memory 2.4 times better than its best physical qubit; the open problem is the remaining limit, since repetition-code runs show errors limited by rare correlated events about once per hour. source

  • Energy cost of erasing a bit

    The Landauer bound, the minimum heat needed to erase a classical bit, has been reached in a one-bit colloidal-particle memory only for long erasure cycles; how close fast, practical logic can come to that bound is open. source

  • Patterning below today's EUV

    ASML describes its TWINSCAN EXE:5000 as the first High NA (0.55) EUV system, with 8 nm resolution aimed first at 2 nm-class logic; making single-exposure patterning at that resolution reliable and economical in volume is the current lithography frontier. source

What this means for training models

No dataset in our catalog sits at this level, and none is filed here. It is on the map because it is where the capacity to train comes from. The size of the models that can be trained, and the cost of running them, are limited by how many transistors fit on a chip and how much energy each one uses, along with memory bandwidth and how chips are connected.
