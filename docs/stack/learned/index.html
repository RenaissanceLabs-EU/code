Learned programs: code that nobody wrote

Is a neural network a program?

A neural network is a program in the strict sense. It is a fixed sequence of arithmetic, mostly multiplications and additions with a few simple curves between them, carried out by ordinary code on ordinary chips. What is different is where it came from.

The structure of the network is written by people and is short: a few hundred lines. The behavior is not in those lines. It is in the weights, millions or billions of numbers that a training loop adjusted, a little at a time, until the outputs matched examples. Nobody chose any one of those numbers, and none of them comes with a comment.

So there is now important software with no source code in the usual sense. It can be run and it can be tested, but it cannot simply be read. Interpretability research is the attempt to read it anyway.

This level sits at the top of the stack because it runs on everything below. It is also reaching back down: models now write code at every level of this map. For a model that writes programs, the forms of a program are both the curriculum and the examiner. Text and tokens are what it reads. Types, tests and proofs are what judge its work.

Seventeen numbers that compute XOR

"Exclusive or" is 1 when exactly one of two inputs is 1. No single artificial neuron can compute it, because one neuron can only split its inputs with a straight line, and XOR needs two. A network with one hidden layer of four neurons can. It has 17 adjustable numbers: 8 weights and 4 offsets going in, 4 weights and 1 offset coming out.

Nobody knows in advance which 17 numbers work. Training finds a set, and a different random start finds a different set that does the same job.

What to notice
  • Before training the answers are noise. After a few hundred steps the four corners come out right.
  • "New random start" ends in different numbers with the same behavior. The program is not unique, and no single number in it means anything by itself.
  • Compare this with the gates level, where XOR is built on purpose from a handful of gates and every wire has a reason.

Central ideas

Weight
One adjustable number in a network. Large language models have billions.
Architecture
The fixed arrangement of arithmetic that the weights plug into. This part is written by people.
Training
Repeatedly nudging the weights in the direction that reduces error on examples.
Loss
The number that measures how wrong the network currently is. Training pushes it down.
Inference
Running the finished network on new input.
Generalization
Working correctly on inputs that were not among the training examples.
Interpretability
The study of what computation a trained network actually performs inside.

Landmarks

  1. 1958
    The perceptron Frank Rosenblatt

    In July 1958 the U.S. Office of Naval Research unveiled Rosenblatt's perceptron, a machine that learned to tell cards marked on the left from those on the right after 50 trials, which the Navy presented as a machine that learns by trial and error. source

  2. 1986
    Backpropagation paper David Rumelhart, Geoffrey Hinton and Ronald Williams

    Their paper Learning representations by back-propagating errors (Nature 323, 1986) described learning representations in multilayer networks by back-propagating errors (earlier work on the method exists, including Werbos). source

  3. 2012
    AlexNet Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever and Geoffrey Hinton

    Their NIPS 2012 network, trained on GPUs, classified 1.3 million ImageNet images into 1,000 classes with 39.7 percent top-1 and 18.9 percent top-5 error, clearly beating the previous best. source

  4. 2017
    Transformer Ashish Vaswani and seven coauthors

    Attention Is All You Need (arXiv June 12, 2017) introduced the Transformer, based entirely on attention, reaching 28.4 BLEU on English to German translation while training in parallel. source

  5. 2018
    Generative pre-training OpenAI

    OpenAI's June 11, 2018 work pre-trained a transformer on unlabeled text with a language-modeling objective and fine-tuned it on specific tasks, with state-of-the-art results on several language tasks. source

  6. 2021
    Codex Mark Chen and coauthors, OpenAI

    Evaluating Large Language Models Trained on Code (arXiv July 7, 2021) reported that Codex solved 28.8 percent of HumanEval problems on one attempt and 70.2 percent with 100 samples per problem, while GPT-3 solved none. source

  7. 2023
    Algorithm recovered from weights Neel Nanda, Lawrence Chan, Tom Lieberum, Jess Smith and Jacob Steinhardt

    The paper (arXiv January 12, 2023) reverse-engineered small transformers trained on modular addition, finding they use discrete Fourier transforms and trigonometric identities to turn addition into rotation on a circle. source

Open problems and frontiers

  • Interpreting what networks compute

    Sharkey and 28 coauthors (January 2025) survey the frontier of mechanistic interpretability and argue it needs conceptual and practical advances before it can meet its scientific and engineering goals. source

  • Verifying learned programs

    Reluplex (2017) verified properties of ACAS Xu collision-avoidance networks about an order of magnitude larger than prior methods; verifying properties of networks at the scale of modern models is open. source

  • Reliability of learned systems

    Kalai and coauthors (2025) argue that language-model hallucinations arise from statistical pressures in pretraining and that benchmarks that penalize uncertain answers teach models to guess. source

What this means for training models

Machine learning code is itself code, and teaching models to write it is an active area: benchmarks ask a model to train a network for a given task, reproduce a result from a paper, or speed up a training script. The checks are execution-based (did the score improve), which makes them slow and noisy next to unit tests.

Everything in the Train section of this site belongs to this level: the trainers, the published recipes and the open models themselves. For how models are trained in general, see Learn.
